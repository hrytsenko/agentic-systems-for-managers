Agentic systems need agents to solve tasks, models to make decisions, and data to ground them. Each topic takes one of them and explains what a large organization sets up once for many teams to run it.

The guide covers agents that do business work: assistants that people work with, such as one that drafts replies to customers, and agents that run on their own inside business processes, such as one that checks each new invoice against its order. Agents across the software development lifecycle (SDLC), from writing code to testing and releasing it, go through the same controls, but their specifics stay out of the guide. It also leaves out how teams build, host, and evaluate their agents, which is software development and needs a guide of its own.

A large organization has to control both where its data goes and what its agents do, because an agentic system sends the data to whoever runs each part and acts on what comes back. When a vendor runs a part, its **data terms** decide what happens to the data: whether it is used for training, how long it is kept, and in which region it is processed. Enterprise agreements usually exclude training and limit retention, and a regional deployment can meet **data residency**, the need to keep data in a given country. Where the terms are enough, the vendor's solution is simpler and usually the better choice. They fall short when regulation or a contract with a customer forbids sending the data to a vendor, or to one that the laws of its home country can force to hand over data stored in another country. They also fall short when systems run on a network with no connection to the internet. Then the organization has to own that part, and only for the work that needs it, because it takes on the cost that a vendor would otherwise carry. Either way, it has to govern its agents, because data terms do not limit what agents do.

Where the organization has to own a part, it needs a team with the expertise to run it. Governed agents need a governance team, together with security, to approve assets and set policies, and platform engineers to run the registry and gateways. Sovereign models need platform engineers who can run models on GPUs. Semantic data needs data engineers to build the pipelines and owners in each department to decide which content is correct and what business terms mean. A vendor's solution can take over much of the platform work, but not the governance or the ownership of data.
