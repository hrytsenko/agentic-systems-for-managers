# Agent instructions

## Vision

A lean guide for managers and staff engineers to talk about enterprise agentic systems and judge what it takes to run them. It gives a clear foundation, not a shallow overview, deep enough to connect business needs with technical approaches. The guide focuses on enterprise capabilities, which a large organization sets up once for many teams, and describes what the organization runs rather than what vendors offer. Pages mention vendors only where a decision depends on them.

The home page sets the scope: agents that do business work, not how teams use agents across the software development lifecycle (SDLC), and not how they build, host, and evaluate agents. Examples and data come from business work, and software work appears only where it concerns agent assets.

## Audience

Managers and staff engineers. They judge whether a customer request fits current resources and expertise, or needs more. A detail stays if it changes the cost, the risk, or who has to own it, and solution details beyond that stay out.

## Content

The guide is split into topics, one page for each part of agentic systems: agents, models, and data. Each topic is self-sufficient: it can be read alone and in any order, so topics do not link to each other, and navigation comes from the sidebar. A topic's title names the demanding version of a capability.

The home page gives a brief intro, the scope, the reasons that span the parts, such as data terms, and the teams that the parts need. A topic repeats such a reason only as a clause, where a decision depends on it.

Within a topic, each term is defined once, where it first belongs, and is bold there. When a topic needs a term that another topic explains in depth, it explains the term only as far as the topic needs and leaves the depth to the other topic. Terms common to the whole guide, such as agent, model, or MCP, need no explanation.

## Topics

A topic is split into an intro that gives the whole picture and subtopics that give the details. The intro defines the topic and names its three or four subtopics in one sentence, with a clause on what each does. Each subtopic is a capability that the organization sets up for many teams: it has real tooling, takes expertise to run, and brings value to the business.

Subtopics follow the order in which the intro names them. Each opens with a short paragraph that explains the capability and repeats its term in bold, then lists its three most important aspects, saying what each is and how it works. The aspects build on each other, and a closing paragraph can explain what spans them. A subtopic ends with a reference product, saying what it covers and, if relevant, what needs a paid edition. Free and open-source software (FOSS) is preferred, but not required.

## Takeaways

A Takeaways tip (`:::tip[Takeaways]`) closes a topic's intro. It is not a summary but the two or three most important takeaways, those that significantly impact decisions or require actions, the most important first. Each is a short bold title that names an action, followed by one short sentence that explains why. Readers may stop after the intro, so each takeaway stands on its own, without terms that only the subtopics explain.
